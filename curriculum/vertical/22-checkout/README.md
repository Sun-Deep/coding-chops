# Vertical 22: why the other line always moves faster

Status: built, not posted

## What it claims

Two shops with the same three tills and the same forty shoppers, walking in at
the same seconds with the same checkout times. On the left everyone joins the
shortest line and stays. On the right there is one shared line, and its front
goes to the next till that comes free.

You join the shortest line behind a checkout that turns out slow. Seven people
who walked in after you are served before you, and you wait 2:53. In the shared
line nobody behind you can be served first, and you wait 0:25.

The catch: on average the shared line only saves 11 percent. It does not make
the shop much faster. It takes the bad luck out of it.

## Why this topic

The second cut built from the Instagram Explore study, after the elevator did
112K. Same shape (two sides, the same people, one orange "you"), a lock every
viewer has stood at, and two systems that both exist, so nobody can call the
left one a straw man the way the elevator's in-order car was called.

It fixes what confused people on the elevator cut: the two rules are said in
the first three seconds, the clocks say what they count, and no line of
narration quotes a number a counter on screen has not reached yet.

## Scope

It teaches that separate lines let later arrivals overtake you whenever a
checkout ahead of you runs long, that one shared line makes that impossible,
and that the average gain is small.

It does not model people switching lines, express lanes, or self-checkout.
Nobody switches here, and that is stated.

"You" is the shopper in seed 1 whose two waits differ most, picked because the
cut is about the bad draw. The payoff does not rest on that pick: across
200,000 simulated shoppers, 27.5 percent were overtaken in the shortest-line
shop and none in the shared one.
