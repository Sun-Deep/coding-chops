# Vertical 33: should you leave a seat between strangers

Status: built, not posted

## What it claims

In a cinema with no assigned seats, if everyone keeps an empty seat between
themselves and strangers, the free seats end up as single gaps all over the
room. A group arriving later finds plenty of seats free and no run long
enough to sit together. If everyone sits right beside whoever is there, the
free seats stay in one piece. Free space broken into pieces too small to
use is what a computer's memory allocator calls fragmentation, and packing
is how allocators avoid it.

In the model, at the defaults, keeping a gap leaves 18.6 of 60 seats free
on average when the first group has to split up; packing leaves 0.7. Over a
full house, 4.6 groups split against 0.2.

## Why this topic

Leaving a seat between strangers is an argument people already have, on
buses and trains as well as in cinemas. The race runs to the end: both
rooms fill one group at a time, the four friends wait at the back from the
first frame, and after they sit the rooms keep filling to a full house. A dark room lit by the screen suits the lit look that the
kiosk reel's follows and shares rewarded.

## Scope and honesty

How people choose seats is not measured from any cinema: the rules are
assumptions, and the script sweeps how many people keep a gap, the mix of
group sizes and the size of the room. The gap between the two rules holds
in every setting. Once the room is full, keeping gaps also leaves more
people beside a stranger (42 against 36.5), because the single gaps fill
with strangers last.

The reel shows seed 1 filled to a full house. The followed four are group
16, the first group of four that cannot sit together when people keep a
gap: both rooms have 23 seats free as it arrives, and the longest free run
is 3 in one and 10 in the other. By the full house three groups are split
up in the first room and none in the second.
