# Vertical 35: should you share the bench while you rest

Status: posted 2026-10-13

## What it claims

One bench at the after-work peak. If whoever has it keeps it through their
rests, sitting on it between sets, the bench is taken but idle most of the
time and everyone else waits for a whole workout to finish. If lifters take
turns, the next one lifts while the last one rests, and the wait falls even
though every turn costs a plate change. A processor does the same: while
one program waits, another runs. That is multitasking, and the swap cost is
a context switch.

In the model, at the defaults, the average wait for the bench falls from
12:44 to 2:43, and the time from walking in to the last set from 22:00 to
13:30. 15.1% of lifters finish later when people take turns, by 1:36 on
average for them, because a turn is not always free the moment their rest
ends.

## Why this topic

Asking to work in, and people sitting on a bench with a phone, are
arguments gym-goers already have, and the viewer is on one side of them.
The race runs to the end: you walk in at frame 0, wait above for the whole
reel, and get the bench below at about 7 seconds. The cause is on screen
the whole time: a lifter sitting on the bench with a phone while people
stand around it.

## Scope and honesty

Arrivals, set lengths, rests and plate-change times are assumptions, not
data from a gym. The script sweeps them. Taking turns cuts the wait at
every crowd, rest, set length and plate-change time tried. On time to
finish, it stops winning when a plate change takes about two minutes,
close to the rest it fills, and the notes say so.

The reel shows seed 1. The followed lifter is #4, whose wait for the bench
when people keep it is the closest in that evening to the 100-evening
average. That lifter gets the bench after 2:03 below and would get it after
11:42 above, which is past the end of the reel.

## How it got here

The first cut ran the clock at about 110 times real time to show the whole
11-minute wait. A set lasted under half a second, people slid between
poses by fading from one drawing to another, and the camera looked along
the bench from its foot, which hid the lift. The creator called it clunky.
This cut runs at 18 times real time, so a set takes about two seconds and a
rest about six; it is seen from the side, where a bench press reads at a
glance; and every person is a jointed figure that sits, lies back, presses,
sits up and walks, routed around the rack. The two waits arrive as the
payoff instead of on a clock that has to cover all of them.
